The runway at night is an electrical system

Airfield Ground Lighting (AGL) is every light, sign and guidance aid on the movement area, plus the regulators, transformers and cable that keep each one at the right brightness. This course covers what pilots see, why the circuits are built the way they are, and how to size, maintain and fault-find them.

DANGER

Series circuits run at kilovolts. A constant current regulator will raise its output voltage to whatever it takes to push the set current, up to its rated maximum (about 4.5 kV for a 30 kVA, 6.6 A unit). Opening a primary circuit or an isolation transformer secondary while energised produces dangerous voltages.

Always isolate the regulator, open the series cutout, lock out, tag out and prove dead before work. This course is training material. It does not replace your aerodrome's procedures, the manufacturer's instructions or the current edition of the standards.

Why AGL uses series circuits

A runway edge circuit can be 5 to 10 km of cable with a hundred lights strung along it. On a parallel (constant voltage) feed, the lights at the far end would see a lower voltage and burn dimmer. Pilots judge distance and alignment from light patterns, so uneven brightness is not acceptable.

In a series circuit the same current flows through every light. A constant current regulator (CCR) holds that current at a set value, typically 6.6 A at full brightness. Every lamp sees the same current, so every lamp has the same intensity regardless of where it sits on the loop.

Each light connects to the loop through its own series isolation transformer. The primary winding is in series with the loop; the secondary feeds the lamp. When a lamp fails, the transformer keeps the loop closed and the rest stay lit.

Model assumptions

Lamp loads are modelled as fixed resistances reflected through transformers of 85 % efficiency. An open-secondary transformer is modelled as a reactive impedance 2.5 times its loaded value, standing in for the saturated magnetising branch. The earth path between two faults is taken as 8 Ω. Real values depend on the transformer, the soil and the regulator, so treat the numbers as trends, not predictions.

Relative intensity per step follows a typical halogen curve: B1 ≈ 0.15 %, B2 ≈ 1.2 %, B3 ≈ 5 %, B4 ≈ 25 %, B5 = 100 %.

The hardware between the switchboard and the lamp

Each piece of equipment in a series circuit has one job and a known failure mode. Knowing both is what lets you read a fault from the vault.

Constant current regulator (CCR)

The CCR takes a constant-voltage supply and delivers a constant current to the loop. It measures output current continuously and adjusts output voltage so the current stays at the selected brightness step, typically within about ±0.1 A at 6.6 A. Load changes (a lamp failing, the cable warming up) change the voltage, never the current.

Rating. A CCR's kVA rating is its rated current times its maximum output voltage. A 20 kVA, 6.6 A regulator can drive up to about 3 030 V. If the loop needs more than that, the CCR can no longer hold the current and the lights dim.

Measuring output. Thyristor CCRs produce a chopped waveform at low steps. Measure output current with a true-RMS clamp meter; an average-responding meter will read wrong.

Open-circuit protection

If the loop opens, current falls to zero and the CCR would drive full voltage into the break. Protection shuts the output down within a few seconds and latches until reset.

Overcurrent protection

Trips if output current exceeds the set step by a set margin, protecting lamps and transformers from a regulation failure.

Insulation monitoring

An insulation resistance monitor (IRMS / earth fault monitor) injects a DC test voltage between the loop and earth and reports the insulation resistance continuously.

Series isolation transformer

HIGH V

Never open a secondary on a live circuit. With the lamp removed, the secondary terminals of a saturated transformer carry high peak voltages. Replace lamps only with the circuit isolated, or where your procedures and equipment (for example IEC 62870 secondary circuit safety devices) explicitly allow live lamp changes.

Each fixture has a transformer with its primary in series with the loop and a secondary feeding the lamp. Typical units are 6.6 A / 6.6 A, sized by lamp load: 10/15, 20/25, 30/45, 65, 100, 150, 200 and 300 W are common ratings.

Lamp failure. When the lamp opens, the secondary has no load. The transformer core saturates, the voltage across its primary rises, and the loop stays closed. The CCR sees a small rise in reactive load and keeps the current where it is. Every other lamp stays lit.

Sizing. Pick a rating at or above the lamp wattage. An overloaded transformer cannot deliver full secondary current, so the lamp runs dim. A very oversized one wastes magnetising current.

Cable, connectors and light bases

Primary cable is single-core 5 kV class (FAA L-824), commonly 8 AWG (8.4 mm²) or 6 mm² copper, shielded or unshielded according to the design. It runs in duct or direct-buried, looping in and out of each light base.

Connectors (FAA L-823) are moulded plug-and-receptacle kits on the transformer leads and cable ends. Field-attached kits must be made up exactly as specified and sealed with heat-shrink; a badly made connector is the most common source of water ingress and earth faults.

Light bases (cans) house the transformer and connectors. FAA L-867 bases suit elevated lights in non-load-bearing areas; L-868 bases are load-bearing for inset lights in pavement.

Lightning protection. A bare copper counterpoise is laid above the duct or cable and bonded to each light base, so lightning currents travel in the counterpoise rather than through the cable insulation.

Records. Label every cable at every pit and keep an as-built record of circuit routing. Fault finding on an unrecorded circuit takes days instead of hours.

LED retrofits. LED fixtures draw far less power but present a non-linear load. Confirm the CCR is compatible, check its minimum-load requirement, and re-check lamp-failure detection, which relies on load changes that are much smaller with LEDs.

The vault: where the airfield lighting is powered

The AGL vault (substation) takes the airport's power supply, keeps it alive through failures, and turns it into the series currents that feed every circuit on the airfield. It is also where most switching, testing and fault finding starts.

    Working safely in the vault

    DANGER

    Many live circuits share one room. The CCR next to the one you are working on may be running, its output reaches several kilovolts, and the interleaved partner of your circuit may pass through the same light bases. Isolate what you will touch, prove it dead, and treat everything else as live.

    1. Agree the work with air traffic control or operations, and confirm which circuits will be dark and for how long.
    2. Switch the circuit to local control at the vault, so the tower or ALCMS cannot switch it on.
    3. Switch the CCR off, open its input breaker on the LV switchboard, and fit your personal lock and tag.
    4. Open the series cutout and prove the field side dead with a suitably rated detector.
    5. For interleaved circuits, identify the partner circuit. Isolate it too if your work touches shared light bases or trenches.
    6. Stand on the insulating mat, wear insulated gloves rated for the voltage, and wear arc-rated clothing for switchboard work as the arc-flash study requires.
    7. To restore: remove locks, close the cutout, run the CCR through every step and check the current, return to remote control and confirm with the tower.

    From operating category to circuit schedule

    The operating category the runway must support sets the lighting systems, their serviceability levels and how fast they must recover from a power failure. Circuit design follows from that.

    Design sequence

    1. Operational requirement. Runway type, approach category (non-precision, CAT I, II, III), lowest RVR, low-visibility taxi routes, and which rule set applies (ICAO, EASA, FAA or national).
    2. Lighting systems. Choose systems and patterns: approach lighting, PAPI, edge, threshold, end, centreline, TDZ, taxiway centreline or edge, stop bars, runway guard lights, signs.
    3. Fixtures. Select certified fixtures for each position and photometric class. Record wattage and transformer rating for each.
    4. Circuits. Group fixtures into circuits by switching need and integrity. Interleave critical systems. Check every circuit's load and voltage against the CCR rating and cable class.
    5. Power supply. Size the AGL substation and secondary power so each system meets its switch-over time.
    6. Control and monitoring. Specify the airfield lighting control and monitoring system (ALCMS), including individual lamp monitoring where serviceability rules demand it.

    Circuit design rules of thumb

    Interleave critical systems

    Feed alternate lights of the approach, runway edge, centreline and TDZ from two circuits on separate CCRs. If one circuit fails, the pattern stays uniform at half density instead of showing a dark gap. Try Lose circuit A on the map.

    One CCR per switching function

    Lights that must be switched or set to a brightness step together share a circuit. Systems switched independently (each stop bar, each taxi route segment) need their own circuit or addressable switching on the circuit.

    Keep within voltage and load

    Load the CCR to roughly 60 to 90 % of rating, leaving room for extensions without running it so lightly that power factor suffers. Keep the step-5 output voltage within the cable's voltage class.

    Route for fault finding

    Bring circuits back to accessible pits so they can be split in half for sectionalising. Avoid running both interleaved circuits through the same duct where one dig-in could cut both.

      Isolate, test, split, repair, prove

      Most AGL faults are water, damaged connectors or failed lamps. The skill is finding which pit, quickly and safely, on a circuit that may be several kilometres long.

      Safe isolation of a series circuit

      1. Get the circuit released by air traffic control or the operations authority. Agree how the area is protected while it is dark.
      2. Set the CCR to off and switch it to local control, so the ALCMS cannot turn it back on.
      3. Open the CCR input supply and lock it out. Open the series cutout or output disconnect between the CCR and the field circuit.
      4. Apply your personal lock and tag to both. Prove the output dead with a suitably rated detector, testing the detector before and after.
      5. After any insulation test, discharge the cable to earth: a few kilometres of cable holds a charge that can bite.
      6. Before re-energising: remove earths, run an insulation test, confirm all personnel are clear of the field circuit, then restore in reverse order.

      Fault-finding scenarios

      Work through each scenario. Choose the next step; the feedback explains why it is right or wrong.

      Find the short

      A runway edge circuit has a short to earth somewhere along its loop. Make it safe, clip the insulation tester on, split the loop at the light bases and test each piece until you find the short. Every action costs the time it would take on the airfield.

      How to play
      1. In the vault, click the CCR input breaker to switch it off and lock it out, then pull the series cutout plug to expose the field OUT and RET terminals.
      2. Drag the red LINE clip onto a conductor and the black EARTH clip onto earth. Pick a test voltage on the dial and press TEST. The reading climbs while the cable charges; press TEST again to stop when it has settled.
      3. Click a base on the map to walk there, click the base plate to open it and click a connector to unplug it. The loop is now split: clip onto the cable side or the transformer side and test each one.
      4. Only the piece that contains the short reads low. Keep splitting it until you know exactly which cable, connector or transformer has failed, then use its Replace or Dig up chip.
      5. Plug everything back in and prove the whole loop from the vault to finish.
      6. Difficulty: Easy has one fault that shows at any test voltage. Medium may hide it as a dry crack that only breaks down at 2500 V or more. Hard has two to five faults, sometimes more than one in the same base, so a section can still read low after you fix one.

      Tip: hold a test for a full minute to get the polarization index. Healthy insulation keeps rising (PI about 2); a wet fault stays flat. Some faults are dry cracks that only show at 2500 V or more. When you open a base, look before you test: a burnt, tracked connector boot or a scorched, swollen transformer gives the fault away. Old tape, rust and mud look bad but are usually harmless, and a buried cable fault cannot be seen at all.

      Event log

        Find the open circuit

        The CCR on a runway edge circuit trips on open circuit and every light is out: the loop is broken somewhere. Make it safe, bond one end of the loop to earth with an earthing jumper, then work along the circuit with the tester on its continuity range until you find the break.

        How to play
        1. In the vault, switch off and lock out the CCR and pull the series cutout plug. Set the tester to Ω and measure from field OUT to field RET: OL confirms the loop is open.
        2. Drag an earthing jumper from the tray onto field OUT. That end of the loop is now bonded to earth, and the jumper stays there when you walk away.
        3. At a base, open it, unplug a connector and measure from the cable side to the can earth. A low reading means the conductor is unbroken all the way back to the jumper; OL means the break is between here and the vault.
        4. Plug the connector back in before you move on, or the next reading will stop at that split. Keep halving the circuit until you know which cable, connector or transformer is open. To test a transformer directly, unplug both sides and measure across its two leads.
        5. Repair it, plug everything back in, take off every jumper and prove the loop from OUT to RET.

        Difficulty: on Easy a broken part inside a base can be seen when you open it. On Medium about half cannot: a burnt-open contact only shows when the connector is unplugged, and a burnt-open winding only shows on the meter. Hard has two or three breaks: from one jumper you can only see as far as the first one, so move the jumper or use a second one from the RET side.

        Event log

          Lighting fault simulator

          The runway edge lighting is live: every light on the map shows whether it is on. Read the pattern of dark lights and the CCR's alarms, then find and fix every fault, from a burnt-out lamp to the CCR itself, and put the lighting back in service.

          How to play
          1. Read the map first. One light out points at that light: lamp, fitting, secondary connector or transformer. Every light on a circuit out points at the CCR or the loop. Every other light out on an interleaved edge is one whole circuit.
          2. In the vault, the CCR shows its step, current and alarms. Clamp the meter on its output to read the true current: a CCR out of calibration still displays 6.6 A. A dark display means no control power: check the control fuse.
          3. To test the CCR itself, lock it out, pull the cutout plug (the CCR side is then shorted) and switch it on. A healthy CCR regulates into the short; one that still trips on open circuit has a faulty control board.
          4. At a light, change the lamp or the fitting from above. Open the base to unplug the secondary connector and measure each side on the continuity range: the lamp side reads about 1.4 Ω, the transformer secondary about 0.3 Ω. Far less means shorted turns.
          5. A single short to earth leaves every light on and only raises the earth fault alarm, because the series loop floats. Two shorts to earth make a path through the ground: the lights between them go dark, while the rest stay on. Find both with the insulation tester.
          6. Interleaved circuits may be fed from two vaults, one at each end of the runway. Where a vault has a standby CCR, lock out and use the changeover switch to put a circuit on the standby: the lighting comes back at once and the duty CCR can be repaired out of circuit.
          7. Lock out before any work and remake every joint and remove every jumper before you switch on again. The job is done when every light is on and every alarm is clear.
